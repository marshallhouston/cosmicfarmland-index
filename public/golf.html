Overview

Every round posted to GHIN, scored from the hole-by-hole cards. Differential is GHIN's own number for the round — lower is better, and it accounts for course rating, slope and playing conditions, so it's the only fair way to compare a 78 at one course to an 84 at another. Where a round is short of 18 holes, this page uses GHIN's scaled-up differential (marked †), which is the value that actually counts toward your index — see How the numbers work.

The climb

GHIN's index isn't a scoring average — it's an algorithm over your differentials, with its own caps, adjustments and revision schedule. So the numbers below are GHIN's own: the index it publishes, and the rounds it flags as currently counting. The target is expressed in differentials, because that's the part a single round actually moves.

To par vs differential — two ways to rank a round

These are not the same measure and they don't produce the same order. To par is what you shot against the card. Differential is GHIN's number: your score adjusted for how hard the course played, which is the only fair way to compare a round at Legacy Ridge to one at Applewood. A round can win on one and lose on the other — which is exactly why both are here. This section uses complete 18-hole rounds only; a nine's to-par isn't comparable, and a short round's differential is partly an estimate rather than a measurement.

How the numbers work

The short version: your index is not a scoring average, and to par is not a differential. Everything below is the World Handicap System as the USGA publishes it, with the formula worked on two of your own rounds.

1 · Adjusted Gross Score

Before anything else, each hole is capped at net double bogey — par + 2 + any handicap strokes you get on that hole. A 9 on a par 4 may enter the calculation as a 7. This is why this page keeps two numbers: outcomes and streaks use your raw score, so a quad reads as a quad, while differentials use GHIN's adjusted score, because that is what the system actually consumes.

2 · Score Differential

One number per round, normalised so any course compares to any other. 113 is the Slope Rating of a course of standard difficulty.

Differential = (113 ÷ Slope) × (Adjusted Gross Score − Course Rating − PCC)

PCC is the Playing Conditions Calculation — a daily adjustment, usually 0, applied when the field as a whole scored unusually high or low.

2b · Scaling short rounds up to 18

A 9-hole round, or an 18 where some holes went unplayed, cannot be compared to a full 18 as-is. GHIN scales it up, and that scaled value is what counts toward your index. The API returns both, and the difference is not small — a 9-hole differential of 5.9 becomes 11.3 once scaled. This page uses the scaled figure everywhere and marks those rounds †, with the raw value shown on the scorecard. Ranking short rounds on their unscaled differential would put them near the top of every leaderboard, which is exactly the mistake this note exists to prevent.

Worth knowing what that scaled number contains. Since the 2024 revision, the holes you didn't play are filled with your expected Score Differential — a neutral value derived from your Handicap Index, not from anything you did on the course. So a scaled round is part measurement, part assumption, and the assumption moves as your index moves. It shows in your own record: the same nine at Silo added +7.3 when your index was 12.9 and +5.4 when it was 8.1. Treat scaled rounds as valid for your index — they are — but weak evidence about how you actually played.

3 · Handicap Index

The average of your lowest 8 Score Differentials out of your most recent 20 rounds. Not all 20 — only the best 8 count, which is why one bad round barely moves it and why a good round only helps if it beats one of the eight already counting. With fewer than 20 scores a sliding table applies:

4 · The safeguards

Your Low Handicap Index is the lowest index you've held in the past year — it's the anchor. A soft cap slows any rise more than 3.0 above it, counting only half of the additional movement. A hard cap stops the rise at 5.0 above it entirely. An exceptional score — well below your index — triggers an extra downward adjustment. These only ever restrain upward movement, never downward.
